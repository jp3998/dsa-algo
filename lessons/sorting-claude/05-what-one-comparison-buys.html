Part I · Order and Information

Lesson 05

What one comparison buys

Where we are

Sorting is now a narrowing problem: start with $n!$ live possibilities, finish with one, using questions that each return one of two answers. We have the starting number. What we do not have is the exchange rate — how much of that field a single question clears away.

1 The wrong unit

Lesson 03 already answered a question that sounds like this one. A comparison settles one pair outright, and sometimes more by chaining. Why is that not the answer?

Because it is measured in the wrong unit. “Pairs settled” and “possibilities eliminated” are different quantities, and there is no fixed rate between them. Settling a pair early in a run kills an enormous number of arrangements; settling one at the very end, when only two candidates remain, kills exactly one.

So we change what we count. From here on the thing being tracked is the candidate set: the arrangements still consistent with every answer received so far.

Definition

At any moment during a run, the candidate set is the collection of arrangements that agree with every answer the algorithm has been given. It starts at all $n!$ of them and the algorithm is finished exactly when it contains one.

This is the honest picture of what an algorithm knows. Not a list of facts — a set of worlds still allowed. And it makes the finish line sharp: an algorithm may stop when the set has one member, and may not stop before.

2 Watching candidates die

Four items have $4! = 24$ arrangements, which is few enough to put all of them on the screen and watch what happens to them.

Two things are worth doing deliberately in Figure 2.1 before reading on.

Get to a single survivor, and notice how few questions it took. Twenty-four candidates, and you almost certainly finished in five or fewer.

Then reset and ask a deliberately bad question — one where the split is very lopsided, or one you have already effectively answered. Watch the candidate count barely move. Both of those questions cost the same. Only one of them bought anything.

3 Every question is a cut

Here is the structure the figure is showing, stated once and precisely, because everything in the next two lessons is built on it.

Take the candidate set as it stands, and pick any two items to compare. Every candidate in the set has an opinion about that pair — it is a complete arrangement, so it puts one of the two first. So the set falls into exactly two groups: those that say $a$ comes first, and those that say $b$ does.

Then the answer arrives, and one group is right. Every candidate in the other group contradicts something we now know, and is gone.

What a comparison does

A comparison partitions the current candidate set into two groups, and the answer discards one of them. Nothing else happens — no candidate is ever added back, and no candidate consistent with the answer is ever lost.

Both halves of that last sentence matter, and it is worth seeing why each is true.

Nothing comes back because answers do not expire. An arrangement ruled out by an earlier answer still contradicts it later.

Nothing correct is lost because the true arrangement agrees with every answer the relation gives — the answers are about it. So the truth is in the surviving group every time, and the set can never shrink to nothing.This is why the algorithm can trust its own state. Whatever else is true, the real arrangement is somewhere in the candidate set — which is what makes reaching a set of size one the same thing as knowing the answer.

4 The best a question can do

Now the question that decides everything. A comparison splits the set in two and we keep one part. How small can we force the surviving part to be?

We do not get to choose which group survives. The relation decides, and it is answering about an arrangement we know nothing about. So when we choose a question, we are choosing a split — and then we are handed one side of it, without a say in which.

Suppose the split is 20 and 4. If the answer sends us to the small side we have done wonderfully. But we cannot count on it, and a claim about what sorting must cost cannot rest on luck. The number we can rely on is the size of the larger group, because that is the one we might be handed.

So: to make the guaranteed survivor small, make the two groups as equal as possible. And the best possible case is a split straight down the middle, leaving half.

The limit on a single question

A comparison cannot be relied on to reduce a candidate set of size $m$ below $m/2$. Whatever pair is chosen, one of the two groups has at least half the candidates in it, and that is the group the answer may select.

Notice how little was assumed. Nothing about the items, nothing about which algorithm is running, nothing about clever choices of pair. The argument is only that two groups covering $m$ candidates cannot both be smaller than $m/2$.

Work Figure 4.1 for a moment and something slightly deflating shows up. At the start, with 24 candidates, several questions split 12 and 12 — perfectly. A few questions in, the best available split is no longer even. By the end you are often choosing between a question that leaves 2 and one that leaves 2, when a perfect split would have left 1.5, which is not a thing.

So halving is a ceiling, not a promise. Sometimes it is available and sometimes it is not. That distinction is going to matter enormously: a ceiling is exactly what you need to prove that something cannot be done quickly, and exactly what you cannot use to prove that something can.

5 From $n!$ down to one

We now have both numbers, and can finally put them together.

Start with $n!$ candidates. Finish with 1. Each question, at very best, halves what is left. So after $k$ questions the candidate set cannot be smaller than

$$\frac{n!}{2^k},$$

and for the algorithm to be finished, that has to have come down to 1. Which means $2^k$ has to have grown to at least $n!$:

$$2^k \ge n!.$$

Stop and look at what that says, because it is the shape of the entire result and it arrived without any algorithm being mentioned. It is a statement about counting: a quantity that at best doubles each step has to catch a quantity that starts enormous.

And now the awkward part. We would like to turn $2^k \ge n!$ into a statement about $k$, and to do that we need to know how big $n!$ actually is — not the feeling from Lesson 04, but a number we can put in an inequality.

We also have a gap of a different kind, and it is more serious than it looks. The argument above tracks one run. An algorithm is not one run — it behaves differently on different inputs, and the run we followed might not be its worst. To make a claim about every input at once we need to see the whole of an algorithm’s behaviour in a single picture.

That picture is the next lesson, and it turns out to be a shape everyone already knows.

The idea in one line

What an algorithm knows is not a list of facts but a set of surviving possibilities — and a comparison is a cut through that set which, at the very best, leaves half.

Check yourself

Six candidates remain, and you compare a pair that splits them 5 and 1. You get lucky: the answer leaves the group of 1. Have you disproved the claim that a question cannot be relied on to leave fewer than half?

No, and the reason is the whole difference between a guarantee and an outcome.

The claim is not that every question leaves at least half. It is that no question can be relied on to leave less. The 5–1 split can turn out beautifully, and it can also leave you with 5 — and you had no way to arrange which.

Put it as a bet. Choosing the 5–1 split is choosing a question whose worst outcome is 5 survivors. Choosing a 3–3 split is choosing one whose worst outcome is 3. If you must succeed no matter what the answer is, the second is better, even though the first sometimes does better.

This distinction runs through the rest of the series and has a name coming in Part IV. For now hold the shape: when you cannot control which branch you get, the branch that matters is the bad one.

You have four items and you ask about the same pair twice. What does the second answer do to the candidate set, and what does that tell you about what a comparison really costs?

Nothing at all — and it still costs a question.

After the first answer, every surviving candidate agrees about that pair. So the split is the entire set against nothing, and the answer discards the empty group. The set is exactly the size it was.

The uncomfortable implication: a comparison is not a unit of progress. It is a unit of cost. What you get for it depends entirely on whether the candidates disagreed about the thing you asked.

And this is not only about literal repeats. Asking about a pair already settled by chaining — you know A before B and B before C, and you ask about A and C — is equally free of information and equally expensive. The whole skill of a good sorting algorithm is never spending a question on something the candidates already agree about.

Why did Section 4 insist that we cannot choose which group survives? Could an algorithm not pick the pair whose answer it expects to be favourable?

It can pick the pair. It cannot pick the answer, and the answer is where the narrowing comes from.

An algorithm chooses which question to ask — that is the whole of its freedom, and it is real. What it cannot do is influence the reply, because the reply is a fact about the input, which was fixed before the algorithm started.

“Expects to be favourable” is worth pulling apart too. Expectation requires a belief about which arrangements are more likely, and in the worst case the input is whatever is least convenient. An algorithm that leans on a favourable guess is fast when the guess holds and slow when it does not.

That is not a dead end — it is a real strategy with real consequences, and Part VI is devoted to it. But for proving what every algorithm must spend, we cannot let the algorithm assume anything about the answer it is about to receive.

The bound says the set cannot be relied on to fall below $n!/2^k$ after $k$ questions. Does it follow that some algorithm actually achieves $n!/2^k$?

No. This is the single most misread step in the whole argument.

What we showed is that halving is the most a question can be relied on to do, so no algorithm can be doing better than $n!/2^k$. That is a floor under the candidate count — a statement about what is impossible.

Whether any algorithm gets close is a completely separate question, and the answer is: not always. Figure 4.1 shows why. Once a few answers are in, an even split may not be available at all — no pair divides the survivors in half, because of how the survivors are shaped.

The general form, which is worth memorising as a habit rather than a fact: showing that nobody can do better than $X$ does not show that anybody achieves $X$. These are two different claims requiring two different arguments, and Part IV gives them two different names. Part V then does the genuinely surprising thing — it produces an algorithm that meets this bound, and that it can be met is not something the bound itself ever promised.

What’s still unanswered

We have $2^k \ge n!$, which is most of a result and not yet usable. Two things are missing.

The first is arithmetic: we need to turn “$2^k$ is at least $n!$” into a statement about $k$, and that needs a real grip on the size of $n!$.

The second is more serious. Everything above follows a single run — one input, one sequence of answers. An algorithm is a rule covering every possible input, and its cost is what it does on the worst of them. We have no way to talk about all of its runs at once.

So before the arithmetic, we need a picture of an entire algorithm rather than one execution of it. That is next — and the argument only becomes a proof once we have it.
