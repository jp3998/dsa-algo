Part I · Order and Information

Lesson 00

What order is

Where we are

This is the beginning, so nothing is established yet. But before we write a single algorithm, there is a word doing an enormous amount of unexamined work in the sentence sort this list — the word order. This lesson takes that word apart. What falls out of it shapes every algorithm in the rest of the series, so it is worth the time.

1 An instruction you cannot carry out

Five things are on a table in front of you: a paperclip, a coffee mug, a phone, a book, a key.

Put them in order.

Your first reaction is probably a question rather than an action. In order of what?

Hold on to that reaction, because it is the whole lesson. “Put them in order” is not something you can do. Something is missing from the instruction, and until it is supplied there is no action available to you — not a hard action, not an easy one, none at all.

Now try a different instruction: put them in order of weight. That one you can carry out. Maybe not quickly — you might need a scale, and the paperclip against the key could be annoying — but you know what is being asked and you would know whether you had succeeded.

The difference between those two requests is small to say and large to understand. It is what the rest of this lesson is about.

2 The order was never in the objects

Four people are standing in front of you. Ask them to line up by height and you get a row. Ask the same four people to line up by age and you get a different row. Figure 2.1 lets you ask them yourself.

Nobody changed between the two requests. The objects were identical. What changed was the question you asked about them, and that alone was enough to produce a completely different row.

This is worth stating flatly, because it cuts against the way we normally talk:

The point

Order is not a property that a collection has. It is a property of a collection together with a rule for comparing its members.

That rule — a way of taking any two members and saying which one comes first — is the missing ingredient from Section 1. It is the thing that turns a pile into a row. We will call it a relation, and it is going to be with us for the whole series.

So when someone says “this list is sorted,” they have said half a sentence. Sorted by what?The half-sentence becomes visible the moment you sort something that is not a number. Sorting a list of people forces you to say by which field; sorting a list of integers does not, because the habit answers for you. For numbers the answer is so habitual that we stop noticing a choice was ever made. “Sorted” means “increasing,” and nobody asks. But the choice was made. It was made silently, by convention, before anyone started thinking.

Whenever you meet a sorting problem that feels confusing, the first thing to check is whether the relation has actually been pinned down, or whether habit has been quietly filling it in.

3 You do not see order — you ask about it

Here is the second question, and it is a different one from the first. Given a row of things: is it in order?

With four people lined up by height you would answer in about a second, and it feels like seeing. It is not. Your eye is doing a great deal of small work very quickly: this one against that one, that one against the next. What feels like a single glance is many tiny questions, and the reason it feels like one act is that you are fast, not that it is one act.

Take the speed away and the atom underneath shows up:

Definition

A comparison is the act of taking two items and asking which one comes first under the relation. It returns one of two answers.

This is the smallest possible act of finding anything out about order. Every piece of knowledge you will ever hold about how a collection is arranged is assembled out of answers to questions of exactly that shape. Figure 3.1 slows one down to human speed so you can watch it happen.

Play with it before reading on. Three things are worth noticing, and the third one is easy to walk straight past.

First. When the row really is in order, you have to ask about every single adjacent pair before you are entitled to say so. Stop one question short and the pair you skipped is exactly the pair that could be wrong. Six questions for seven items, and not one fewer.

Second, and stranger. When the row is not in order, you can often find out far sooner. One bad pair settles it. You might hit it on the second question and be finished, with five questions you never had to ask.

That asymmetry is not a quirk of this particular puzzle. Showing that something is broken takes one counterexample. Showing that something is correct takes an argument covering every case, and there is no way to cover every case while leaving one of them unexamined. We are going to meet this same asymmetry again in a much heavier form, when we ask not how much work sorting takes but how much it must take.

Third. At the end — all six questions asked, verdict delivered, certainty achieved — you still do not know a single one of the seven values. You never found out what anything is. You only ever found out how things sit relative to each other, and it turned out that was enough to answer the question you had.This turns out to be a deliberate and very productive restriction rather than an accident of the figure. We make it precise in Lesson 03, and in Part XI we take it away again and watch what becomes possible.

Keep that third observation somewhere safe. A surprising amount of what follows is an investigation of how much can be achieved while knowing only relations and never values.

4 Being in order, and knowing it

Open a fresh deck of cards. It comes out of the box arranged by suit and rank. Shuffle it once, properly. By some spectacular accident, it comes out in exactly the arrangement it started in.

Is the deck sorted?

The cards are in order. That is a fact about the deck, and it is true whether or not anybody ever checks. But you do not know it. If you had to bet on it, or act on it, or hand the deck to someone who needed it sorted, “it might be” is worth nothing at all.

So there are two different things here, and ordinary language smears them together:

  • the arrangement being in order — a fact about the world;
  • knowing the arrangement is in order — a fact about you.

The first costs nothing, because it is already either true or false. The second has to be bought, and the currency is questions.Sharpening this split is what a great deal of computer science turns out to be about. Producing an answer and being able to justify an answer are separate problems, and they do not always cost the same. That purchase is the subject of this entire series. When we eventually prove that a certain amount of work is unavoidable, what we will really be proving is that a certain amount of knowledge cannot be bought more cheaply.

Here is the sharpest way to put it, and it is the sentence to carry out of this lesson.

The real problem

A sorting algorithm’s difficulty is not producing an ordered arrangement. Any arrangement at all can be produced by moving things around. Its difficulty is having a reason — being able to stand behind the claim that the arrangement it produced is the ordered one.

An algorithm that shuffles at random and happens to land on the right answer has not sorted anything. It has guessed, and the next time it runs the guess will be wrong, and nothing in it can tell the difference between the two occasions. The reason is the product. The arrangement is only what the reason is about.

5 What this series is actually about

We now have enough to state the idea that the whole series hangs from. It will look like an overstatement on first reading, and by the end it will look like the plainest possible description of the subject.

The thesis

Sorting is the acquisition, representation, maintenance and exploitation of information about order.

Four verbs, and each of them names a stretch of the road ahead:

  • Acquisition — comparisons, and how much each one buys. That is where Part I goes, and it ends in a limit on how cheaply anyone can buy enough.
  • Representation — how a partly-solved arrangement holds what has been learned so far, so that it is not learned twice.
  • Maintenance — keeping what is known true while the arrangement keeps changing underneath. This is where invariants come from, and it is the hardest of the four.
  • Exploitation — spending what is known to avoid questions that no longer need asking. Nearly every clever algorithm in the series is an act of exploitation.

Once that lens is in place, every algorithm we meet stops being a procedure to memorise and becomes an answer to the same six questions:

  • What do I know?
  • What do I need to know?
  • What information can I obtain, and at what price?
  • What assumptions am I allowed to exploit?
  • What structure can I maintain?
  • What work is unavoidable no matter how clever I am?

You are not expected to be able to use those questions yet. They are here so that you recognise them when they start showing up, which will be almost immediately.

The idea in one line

Order is a question you ask about pairs, not a property you observe in a pile — and knowing that a collection is in order is something you have to buy, one question at a time.

Check yourself

Someone hands you seven items and says “these are in order.” You want to be certain. What is the smallest number of adjacent pairs you could check? Could fewer ever be enough?

Six, and fewer is never enough.

Six is enough: check each neighbouring pair, and if every one comes back good then no two neighbours are out of place, which is what being in order means.

The interesting half is why five can never do. Suppose you check every adjacent pair except the one at positions k and k+1. Now consider the arrangement that is identical to yours except that those two items are swapped. Every question you asked returns exactly the same answer on both arrangements, because none of your questions touched that pair. So the answers you hold cannot distinguish them — and one of the two is out of order. You are not entitled to a verdict.

This shape of argument — construct a second possibility your questions cannot rule out — is the seed of every lower bound in this series. We will do it properly, and much more powerfully, in Lesson 06.

In Figure 3.1 you only ever compared adjacent pairs — six questions, not the twenty-one it would take to compare every item against every other. Why was that enough?

Because you assumed that “comes before” carries along a chain: if the first item comes before the second, and the second before the third, then the first comes before the third — without anyone checking that pair directly.

That assumption feels too obvious to bother stating, which is precisely why it is worth catching yourself using it. You got fifteen answers for free, and you never paid for them. They came from the structure of the relation rather than from any question you asked.

It is not automatic. There are perfectly ordinary relations where it fails, and when it fails this entire procedure collapses — checking neighbours tells you nothing about the whole. Building such a relation and watching the collapse is the first thing we do in Lesson 01.

For now the thing to take away is smaller and more useful: you used a property of the relation without noticing, and free knowledge came out of it. Both halves of that sentence matter later.

Can colours be put in order?

The question as asked has no answer, and recognising that is the point — it is Section 1’s incomplete instruction wearing a different coat.

By wavelength: yes, cleanly. Every pair has a definite answer and the answers chain, so a single row exists.

By how much you like them: now it depends entirely on what your preferences happen to look like. You might prefer red to blue, blue to green, and green to red. There is nothing incoherent about holding those three preferences — people hold them all the time — but no row can be built from them. Try it: whichever colour you put first, one of your own preferences says something else should have come before it.

So “can these things be ordered?” is never a question about the things. It is a question about the relation you are proposing to use, and some relations do not deserve the name. Which ones do is exactly the subject of the next lesson.

Why can you sometimes conclude “not in order” after two questions, but never conclude “in order” before six?

Because the two verdicts are claims of different shapes.

“Not in order” is a claim that some pair is wrong. A single bad answer is a complete proof of it. Nothing you might learn later can undo it, so you can stop.

“In order” is a claim that every pair is right. It is six claims at once, and each one is settled only by its own answer. Five answers leave the sixth claim exactly as open as it was before you started.

The general form: a claim about some case is cheap to confirm and expensive to refute; a claim about every case is cheap to refute and expensive to confirm. Sorting is relentlessly of the second kind, which is a large part of why it costs what it does.

One friend shuffled a deck and it came out in perfect order. Another friend checked every adjacent pair and reports the deck is in perfect order. The two decks are in identical arrangements. Is there any difference between the situations?

None in the decks. All the difference is in the friends.

Both decks are in order — that fact is settled, identical, and was settled before either friend spoke. What differs is what each of them possesses. The first has an ordered deck. The second has an ordered deck and a reason.

The test is what happens when you need to rely on it. Hand both decks to someone who will be harmed if the deck is not sorted: the second friend can tell you why it is safe, and the first can only tell you it worked out.

Everything we build from here exists to manufacture the second condition. And the cost of an algorithm — the number we will spend the rest of the series deriving, bounding and arguing about — is the price of that reason.

What’s still unanswered

We said order comes from a relation — a rule for comparing two things. Then, in Figure 3.1, we leaned hard on a specific property of that rule without ever saying so: that checking neighbours is enough, because “comes before” carries along a chain.

Not every rule does that. Some loop back on themselves. Some leave pairs with no answer at all. Hand either kind to the same procedure and what comes out is not a row you can trust — and the failure is not a bug in the procedure.

So which rules actually produce an order, and which only look as though they do? That is the next lesson, and we find out by breaking things.
