Lesson 04

Counting the arrangements

Where we are

We know what an algorithm may ask and roughly what an answer buys: one pair outright, sometimes more by chaining, never a value. What we have no grip on is the size of the job. To say how many questions sorting takes, we need to know how much there is to find out — and that turns out to be a counting problem with a very specific answer.

1 What exactly are we counting?

An algorithm starts out knowing nothing about the arrangement and finishes knowing it completely. So the thing to measure is how many arrangements it had to choose between.

We already have the right word. Lesson 02 called a rearrangement of the input a permutation, and observed that making it an object of its own would let us count it. Here is where that pays.

Assume for this lesson that the $n$ items are all different, with no ties. Ties reduce the count — they make some arrangements indistinguishable — so counting without them gives the largest the answer can be, which is what a claim about the hardest case needs.

The question

Given $n$ distinct items, how many different orders can they be put in?

We are not going to look the answer up. Looking it up would give a formula, and a formula is the least useful part of a result — it tells you what is true without telling you why, and when you meet a variant it leaves you helpless. So we count.

2 Counting by hand, for as long as we can

With one item there is one arrangement, and nothing to say about it.

With two items — call them A and B — there are two: A then B, or B then A. Still nothing interesting.

With three the work starts, and rather than list them at random, list them in a way that shows where they come from. Every arrangement of A, B, C contains inside it an arrangement of A and B alone, with C inserted somewhere. There are two arrangements of A and B, and for each of those, three places C could go: before both, between them, or after both.

Two times three. Six.

Figure 2.1 runs that process for you, one item at a time.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because the whole lesson rests on it. To go from arrangements of $k$ items to arrangements of $k+1$, take each arrangement you already have and insert the new item into every gap. A row of $k$ items has $k+1$ gaps — one before each item, and one at the end.

Two things need checking before we trust it, and both are easy to skip.

Nothing is missed. Any arrangement of $k+1$ items, with the new item deleted, is some arrangement of the $k$ old ones — so it appears among the children of that one.

Nothing is counted twice. Two children of the same parent differ in where the new item sits. Two children of different parents differ once you remove the new item. Either way they are different arrangements.

3 The pattern, and why it is a product

So each step multiplies. One arrangement of a single item becomes two, then those two become six, then those six become twenty-four:

$$1 \;\rightarrow\; 1 \times 2 \;\rightarrow\; 1 \times 2 \times 3 \;\rightarrow\; 1 \times 2 \times 3 \times 4 \;\rightarrow\; \dots$$

There is a second way to see the same product, and holding both is worth more than holding either.

Instead of growing arrangements, build one arrangement by making choices. Which item goes first? Any of the $n$. Having fixed that, which goes second? Any of the $n-1$ that are left. Then $n-2$, and so on down to the last position, where one item remains and there is no choice at all.

Every distinct sequence of choices gives a distinct arrangement, and every arrangement comes from exactly one sequence of choices. So the number of arrangements is the number of ways to make the choices, which is the product of the number of options at each step:

$$n \times (n-1) \times (n-2) \times \dots \times 2 \times 1.$$

The two derivations disagree about which end to start from and produce the same product, which is a reassuring sign. The first tells you how the arrangements are structured — nested, each built from a smaller one. The second tells you how one is specified — as a sequence of independent decisions. Both views return. The nesting — a big arrangement built out of a smaller one — is how Part V will attack the problem; the sequence of decisions is how Lesson 06 will picture a single run.

Now, and only now, the name:

Definition

For a whole number $n \ge 1$, the product $n \times (n-1) \times \dots \times 2 \times 1$ is written $n!$ and read “$n$ factorial”. By convention $0! = 1$, there being exactly one way to arrange nothing.

What we have

There are exactly $n!$ arrangements of $n$ distinct items.

4 How big is that, really?

A formula is not an intuition. $n!$ tells you how to compute the number; it does not tell you what kind of number it is. And the kind matters enormously for what comes next, so it is worth building the feeling now rather than being surprised later. Figure 4.1 puts it beside the growth everyone already has intuitions about.

Some landmarks, to make it concrete. A deck of cards has 52 arrangements-worth of items, and $52!$ is roughly $8 \times 10^{67}$. That is more than the estimated number of atoms in our galaxy. Shuffle a deck properly and the arrangement you are holding has, with near certainty, never existed before in the history of the world.

The thing to take from this is not that the numbers are large. It is how they get large. Doubling — multiplying by 2 each step — is the usual picture of runaway growth. Factorial multiplies by a number that itself keeps growing. It leaves doubling behind and does not come back.

Hold this loosely for now

We are going to need to say how big $n!$ is in a way that can be used in an argument, rather than only felt. That is a separate job and it gets its own lesson. For now the intuition is enough: it is enormous, and it grows faster than repeated doubling.

5 The job, restated

We can now say what a sorting algorithm is doing in a way that will carry us through the rest of Part I.

Before it asks anything, every one of the $n!$ arrangements is a live possibility. The input might be in any of them, and nothing the algorithm holds rules out a single one. When it finishes, it must have committed to one. Not narrowed things down usefully — committed, with nothing left open, because an output is one specific arrangement.

The job

Sorting is the task of going from $n!$ live possibilities down to exactly one, using questions that each return one of two answers.

Read that twice, because it has quietly become a different problem from the one we started with. It says nothing about items, or positions, or moving things around. It is a statement about narrowing a set of possibilities, and the items have dropped out entirely.

That change of viewpoint is the most valuable thing in Part I. A question about rearranging objects is hard to reason about in general — there are too many ways to rearrange things. A question about narrowing a set of $n!$ candidates using two-way questions is something we can attack directly, because now both sides are numbers.

We have one of those numbers. The next lesson gets the other.

The idea in one line

There are $n!$ arrangements because each new item can be dropped into any gap of an existing one — and sorting is therefore not really about moving items, but about cutting a field of $n!$ candidates down to a single survivor.

Check yourself

Why does a row of $k$ items have $k+1$ gaps rather than $k-1$?

Because the two ends count.

Between $k$ items there are $k-1$ spaces. But the new item can also go before everything, or after everything, and those are two more places — giving $k+1$.

It is worth checking that the ends are genuinely distinct options rather than a technicality. With two items A, B there are three gaps, and the three results are CAB, ACB, ABC. All different, all legitimate arrangements. Lose the ends and you would get 2 instead of 6 at the next step, and every number after that would be wrong.

This is the standard way an off-by-one error destroys a counting argument, and the standard defence is the one used here: do the smallest case by hand and check the count against the list.

The lesson assumed the items are all distinct. If two of the $n$ items are identical, is the number of distinguishable arrangements larger or smaller than $n!$, and by how much?

Smaller — exactly half.

Take any arrangement and swap the two identical items. The result is a different permutation — the positions changed — but you cannot tell the two apart by looking, because the things that moved are the same thing. So the $n!$ permutations fall into pairs that look alike, and the number of distinguishable arrangements is $n!/2$.

Why this matters for the rest of Part I. Everything we are about to prove is about how much has to be learned, and ties reduce how much there is to learn. So the distinct case is the hardest case, which is precisely the one a claim about worst behaviour needs to be about. Assuming distinctness is not a convenience — it is the honest choice.

Worth connecting back: this is the counting version of what Lesson 01 found. Ties mean several arrangements are equally correct, so less has to be determined to reach one of them.

Roughly how many times larger is $12!$ than $10!$? Answer without computing either.

132 times.

$12! = 12 \times 11 \times 10!$, so the ratio is $12 \times 11 = 132$. Nothing else is needed, and the enormous shared factor never has to be evaluated.

The habit is the point. Factorials are almost always easier to handle as ratios than as values, because neighbouring factorials share nearly all of their factors. Comparisons between them are small numbers even when the factorials are astronomical.

Notice also what the answer says about growth. Going from 10 items to 12 — two more items — multiplies the number of arrangements by 132. Doubling would have multiplied it by 4. That gap is the whole content of Section 4, in one arithmetic fact.

Section 5 claimed that when an algorithm finishes it must have narrowed the field to exactly one arrangement. Is that too strong? Could it stop with two still possible and pick one?

It could pick one, but then it is not a sorting algorithm. The distinction is the one Lesson 00 ended on, and it is worth being exact about.

Suppose two arrangements remain and the algorithm outputs one. On some inputs it will be right — half of them, if it guesses well. But an algorithm must be correct on every input, and there is an input consistent with everything it learned for which its output is wrong. So it fails the specification.

The sharper way to put it: what must be reduced to one is not the algorithm’s confidence but the set of possibilities consistent with the answers it received. If two arrangements are both consistent with everything it heard, it has no basis for preferring either, and the world is free to have been the other one.

This is exactly the argument from Lesson 03’s last exercise, and the one Lesson 06 turns into the main result of this part. Third appearance, and it has not finished.

What’s still unanswered

We have the size of the job: $n!$ possibilities down to one. And we have the tool: a question with two possible answers.

What we have not done is put them together, because we do not yet know what a question is worth. Lesson 03 said a comparison settles one pair directly and sometimes more by chaining — true, and useless here, since “pairs settled” and “possibilities eliminated” are different currencies.

So the next question is the one that converts between them. When you ask a comparison, how many of the surviving arrangements does the answer kill?
