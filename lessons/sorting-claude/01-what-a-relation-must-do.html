Part I · Order and Information

Lesson 01

What a relation must do

Where we are

Last lesson we found that order comes from a relation, and then leaned on that relation behaving well without ever saying what “well” means. Now we collect the debt. We are going to take three relations that each look perfectly reasonable, try to build a row out of each, and watch each one fail in a different way. Every failure names one thing a relation has to guarantee — and when we have all three, we can prove something worth having.

1 What a row has to promise

Before we can say a relation fails, we need to be precise about what success would look like. So: what is a row, exactly?

Write the relation as $a \prec b$, read as “a comes before b.”We use a bare symbol rather than $<$ on purpose. The relation might be height, or age, or alphabetical position, or something with no numbers in it anywhere. Nothing in this lesson assumes the items are numbers. A row is an arrangement of all the items, one after another, and what we want from it is this:

What we want

An arrangement is a valid row when, for every pair of items, the one that appears earlier in the arrangement is the one the relation says comes first.

That is a demanding promise, and it is worth seeing how demanding. It is not enough for neighbours to be in the right order relative to each other. Every pair has to agree with the relation, including items sitting at opposite ends with five things between them.

Now we can go hunting. We want relations for which such an arrangement exists, and we want to know that the relation picks out which arrangement it is. The fastest way to understand a requirement is to watch what happens when it is missing, so for the next three sections we break things on purpose.

2 Failure one: the loop

Rock beats scissors. Scissors beats paper. Paper beats rock. Take “beats” as our relation — the winner comes first — and try to build a row.

There are only six possible arrangements of three items, so rather than argue in the abstract we can work through every one of them. Figure 2.1 lets you do it by hand.

Every arrangement fails, and notice how it fails. There is nothing subtle going on, no near-miss we could patch by being cleverer. Whichever item we put first, one of the three facts says something else should have come before it. The relation is fighting itself.

What is the defect? It is not that any single fact is wrong. Each of the three is fine on its own. The trouble is that they chain into a circle: following “beats” three times brings you back where you started.

And a circle is incompatible with a row for a simple reason. In a row, being earlier is a one-way street — if you walk forward you never return to where you began. A relation containing a loop is asking to be laid out along a line while insisting that one of its journeys comes home. No arrangement can satisfy both.

So here is the first requirement. We need chains to go somewhere rather than curl up:

Definition

A relation is transitive when, for all items $a$, $b$ and $c$: if $a \prec b$ and $b \prec c$, then $a \prec c$.

Transitivity is exactly what rock-paper-scissors lacks. Rock beats scissors and scissors beats paper, so a transitive relation would demand that rock beats paper. It does not, and that single broken link is the whole problem.

It is also worth seeing what transitivity gives you, because that is the half people usually miss. Look back at Figure 3.1 in Lesson 00 — the row of hidden items where six questions about neighbours settled the whole arrangement. Those six answers only covered six of the twenty-one pairs. The other fifteen came from chaining, free of charge.

That is not a small convenience. It is the reason sorting is possible at a sane price at all. A relation that made you pay for all twenty-one would be a different and much more expensive problem, and we will see exactly how much more expensive in Lesson 04.

3 Failure two: the two-way pair

The second failure is smaller and takes only two items to produce.

Suppose our relation reports both $a \prec b$ and $b \prec a$. Perhaps it is comparing restaurants and the rule is “whichever I would rather eat at tonight,” and honestly, both.

Which goes first in the row?

The question has no answer, and it has no answer in a way that cannot be repaired. Put $a$ first and the fact $b \prec a$ says that was wrong. Put $b$ first and $a \prec b$ says the same. There is no third option to try. With the loop in Section 2 we at least had six candidate arrangements to work through; here there are two, and both are refuted by the relation itself before we start.

So the second requirement forbids exactly this:

Definition

A relation is asymmetric when $a \prec b$ and $b \prec a$ are never both true.You will often meet the closely related antisymmetry, stated for relations like $\le$ that allow an item to relate to itself: if $a \le b$ and $b \le a$ then $a = b$. It is the same idea dressed for a relation that includes equality. We use the strict form because “comes before” is the question an algorithm actually asks.

It is tempting to file this one under “too obvious to need saying.” Resist that. The restaurant relation is a real relation that real people use, and it breaks here. So does “is at least as tall as,” the moment two people are the same height. Whenever a relation is built from a judgement rather than a measurement, this is the first place it tends to give way.

4 Failure three: the missing answer

The third failure is the most interesting, because at first it does not look like a failure at all.

You are getting dressed. Socks have to go on before shoes. A shirt has to go on before a jacket. That is the entire relation — two facts, no loops, no two-way pairs. It passes both tests we have built so far.

Now build the row. Figure 4.1 gives you all four items and both constraints.

Nothing failed. That is the trouble. Socks before shoes, shirt before jacket — and eight different arrangements keep both promises. The relation is perfectly consistent and it has no preference among them.

The defect is that some pairs have no answer. Ask the relation whether socks come before the shirt and it says nothing at all. Not “no” — nothing. The two items are incomparable, and since the relation has no opinion about where they sit relative to each other, neither can we.

Take a moment on why this is fatal for us specifically, because in other settings it is fine. Lesson 00 ended with the difference between a collection being in order and someone knowing it. Here there is no single ordered arrangement to know about. An algorithm asking comparisons could work forever and never be led to one answer rather than another, because the relation it is questioning does not contain the answer. The problem is not hard; it is underspecified.

Hence the third requirement:

Definition

A relation is total when, for every pair of distinct items $a$ and $b$, exactly one of $a \prec b$ and $b \prec a$ holds.

Read that carefully: exactly one does two jobs at once. “At least one” is totality proper, ruling out the missing answer. “At most one” is the asymmetry from Section 3. Stating it this way bundles both, which is convenient and is why you will often see it written as one condition.

Not a defect, elsewhere

A relation that is transitive and asymmetric but not total is called a partial order, and it is a perfectly good object — the dressing constraints are a real and useful description of the world. It is only a failure relative to the job of producing one determined row.

Hold on to this. In Part VII we deliberately build a structure that maintains a partial order and refuses to learn any more, because the missing answers are precisely the ones we can avoid paying for. What looks like a defect here turns into a saving there.

5 The three together, and what they buy

Three failures, three requirements. Collect them:

Definition

A relation $\prec$ on a set is a strict total order when it is:

  • transitive — $a \prec b$ and $b \prec c$ force $a \prec c$;
  • asymmetric — never both $a \prec b$ and $b \prec a$;
  • total — for distinct $a$ and $b$, one of $a \prec b$, $b \prec a$ holds.

Each was forced on us by a specific way of failing, which is the useful way to hold them. Not three axioms handed down, but three repairs, each patching a hole we watched open.

Now the payoff. We have been assuming a good relation produces a row. Let us actually prove it — and prove something slightly stronger that we are going to need in the very next lesson.

Claim

Let $S$ be a finite, non-empty set and let $\prec$ be a strict total order on $S$. Then there is exactly one valid row of the items of $S$.

Before the proof, the part that is usually left out: how would you find this argument yourself?

Start by asking what the hardest decision is. Building the row is a sequence of choices, and the first one looks like the worst, because it seems to require knowing about everything at once. So make that the question: is there always something that goes first? If we can settle that, the rest of the row is the same problem on a smaller set, and we can lean on that.

Something that goes first means something with nothing before it. So: does such an item always exist? Try to find one by walking. Pick any item. If something comes before it, walk there. If something comes before that, walk there. Either you stop — and where you stop is an item with nothing before it, which is what we wanted — or you walk forever. On a finite set, walking forever means revisiting. And revisiting is exactly the loop that transitivity and asymmetry forbid. So the walk stops, and it stops where we need it to.

That is the whole idea. The proof below is that paragraph written carefully.

Proof

A first item exists. Pick any $a_1 \in S$. If some item comes before $a_1$, call it $a_2$ and repeat; this produces $\dots \prec a_3 \prec a_2 \prec a_1$. By transitivity, every item in this chain comes before every item listed after it. By asymmetry, no item can appear twice — if $a_i$ appeared again later, we would have both $a_i \prec a_i$ and its reverse. So the chain has no repeats, and since $S$ is finite it must stop. It stops at an item $m$ with nothing before it.

That first item is unique. Suppose $m$ and $m'$ both have nothing before them, with $m \neq m'$. Totality says one of $m \prec m'$, $m' \prec m$ holds. Either way one of them has something before it, which contradicts how they were chosen. So $m = m'$.

Induction. Remove $m$ from $S$. The remaining set is finite, and $\prec$ restricted to it is still transitive, asymmetric and total — removing items cannot create a loop, a two-way pair, or a missing answer. So by induction on the size of $S$ it has exactly one valid row. Put $m$ at the front of it. Every item of $S$ other than $m$ has $m$ before it, so the result is a valid row of $S$.

Only one. Any valid row must begin with an item that has nothing before it, since anything before it would have to appear earlier and there is no earlier. We showed that item is $m$, uniquely. The rest of the row is a valid row of $S$ without $m$, which by induction is unique. So the whole row is unique.

That claim is the licence for everything that follows. It is why the phrase the sorted arrangement is legitimate rather than sloppy — with a strict total order there is exactly one thing it could refer to. Without it, “sort this” would not be a question with an answer.

Notice also where finiteness was used: one place only, to force the walk to stop. That is not a technicality, and the exercises ask you to break it.

6 Ties, and the return of many answers

One more thing, and it is the one that survives into the rest of the series.

Sort four people by height: 158, 167, 167, 181. Two of them are the same height.

Under a strict total order this situation cannot arise, because totality demands that for any two distinct people one comes before the other. Our two 167s refuse to comply. They are distinct people — different names, different lives — and the relation we chose cannot separate them.

We could declare this an error and insist on relations that never tie. That would be tidy and would throw away most of the useful relations in the world — sorting by surname, by date, by price, by score all produce ties constantly. So instead we loosen the requirement, carefully.

Keep transitivity, because loops are still fatal. Keep the ban on two-way pairs. But allow a third possibility for a pair: neither comes before the other, and they are not the same item. Call them tied. A relation of this kind is a weak order.

What does that cost? Go back and find the step in the proof that breaks.

It is the uniqueness of the first item. The argument was: if $m$ and $m'$ both have nothing before them, totality forces one before the other, contradiction. With ties allowed, totality no longer forces anything — $m$ and $m'$ can peacefully both be first-eligible. The existence half of the proof survives untouched. The uniqueness half does not.

What we are left with

Under a weak order, a valid row still always exists. It is no longer unique: two items that tie can appear in either order, and every resulting arrangement is equally valid.

Figure 6.1 is that claim in miniature. Both arrangements are right, and nothing in the relation prefers one.

Which leaves a question we are deliberately not going to answer yet. When several arrangements are all correct, does it matter which one you produce? The relation says no. Keep an eye on whether you believe that.

The idea in one line

No loops, no two-way pairs, no missing answers — three guarantees, each one forced by a specific way of failing, and together exactly enough to turn a relation into a single determined row. Allow ties and you keep the row but lose the word single.

Check yourself

Rock-paper-scissors failed to give a row. Exactly which of the three requirements does it break — and does it break any of the others?

Transitivity, and only transitivity. Checking the other two is the point of the exercise.

Asymmetric? Yes. Rock beats scissors and scissors does not beat rock. The same for the other two pairs. No pair goes both ways.

Total? Yes. Take any two of the three and exactly one beats the other. Every pair has an answer.

Transitive? No. Rock beats scissors and scissors beats paper, so transitivity would require rock to beat paper. Paper beats rock.

This is worth registering because it shows a single missing requirement is enough to destroy the row entirely. The relation is two-thirds of the way to being a total order and gets nothing for it.

Take “is a prefix of” on strings, so that car comes before cart. Which requirement fails, and what kind of object is it?

Totality fails; the other two hold. It is a partial order.

Transitive: if a is a prefix of b and b of c, then a is a prefix of c. Asymmetric: two different strings cannot each be a prefix of the other, since a proper prefix is strictly shorter.

But cat and dog are incomparable — neither is a prefix of the other, and the relation has nothing to say about them. This is Section 4 exactly: consistent, useful, and unable to determine a row.

Worth noticing: this is not how dictionaries order words. Dictionary order is a different relation built on top, which handles cat against dog by looking at the first position where they differ. That relation is total, which is precisely why a dictionary can exist as a single sequence of pages.

The proof used finiteness in exactly one place. Find it, then break it: give a strict total order on an infinite set with no valid row in our sense.

The place: the walk. We argued that following “something comes before this” must eventually stop, because a non-stopping walk on a finite set would have to revisit an item, and revisiting is a loop. On an infinite set the walk can continue forever without ever repeating, so the argument evaporates.

The counterexample: the integers under the usual $<$. It is transitive, asymmetric and total, so it is a strict total order. But there is no first integer: whatever you propose, subtract one. The walk $\dots \prec -3 \prec -2 \prec -1$ runs forever.

So no arrangement with a first item, then a second, and so on can list the integers in increasing order. The relation is impeccable; what fails is the attempt to lay it out as a row with a beginning.

This is not a trick. It is why sorting is always posed on a finite collection, and why we will never have to worry about the case again — but it is much better to know which step was carrying that weight than to carry a vague sense that finiteness matters somewhere.

Suppose someone hands you a relation and promises it is transitive and total, but you suspect they are wrong. You can only ask it about pairs. How would you catch a violation of transitivity, and why is that harder than catching a two-way pair?

A two-way pair takes two questions. Ask about $a$ against $b$, then $b$ against $a$. If both come back “comes before,” you are finished.

A transitivity violation needs three items at once. You have to find $a$, $b$, $c$ with $a \prec b$, $b \prec c$ and $c \prec a$. No question about a single pair can reveal it, because every individual answer is perfectly innocent — rock beats scissors is not a suspicious fact. The defect only exists in the combination.

And that is the cost: pairs number around $n^2/2$, but triples number around $n^3/6$. Checking every pair for a two-way violation is far cheaper than checking every triple for a loop.

The general lesson, which will keep returning: how much of the structure a defect touches decides how much work it takes to find. Local defects are cheap to detect. A defect that only exists in the relationship between three separate places is not.

Two people are both 167 cm, and Figure 6.1 shows both arrangements are correct. Is that a flaw in our specification of a row, or a flaw in the relation we chose?

Neither is broken — but the question is the right one to be suspicious about, and it is the one the next lesson starts from.

The relation is not flawed. “Shorter than” is a genuine relation, and the two people genuinely are the same height. Demanding that it separate them would be demanding that it report something untrue.

The specification is not flawed either. It asks that every pair appear consistently with the relation, and both arrangements do.

What has happened is that the specification is silent where the relation is silent. It admits several answers because the relation admits several, and it has no view about which you produce.

Whether that silence is acceptable depends on something outside the relation entirely — on whether the two tied items differ in some way you care about. Two people of the same height are still two different people. If they arrived in a particular order, you might have expectations about the order they leave in. The relation cannot have an opinion about that, because you never told it about anything but height.

Keep that thought. It comes back with a name in Part V, and by Part XI an entire algorithm will depend on it.

What’s still unanswered

We can now say what a relation has to do, and we proved that a good one picks out exactly one row — or, with ties, a family of equally good ones. That settles what order means.

It does not settle what sorting means. Those are not the same question, and the gap between them is wider than it looks. We have been talking as though the job is to arrange items we already hold, but nothing so far says the output has to contain the same items as the input at all. As stated, a procedure that threw everything away and handed back three numbers in increasing order would satisfy every condition in this lesson.

Everyone can see that is wrong. Saying precisely why it is wrong turns out to be the whole of the next lesson — and the second half of the answer is a mathematical object we will lean on for the rest of Part I.
