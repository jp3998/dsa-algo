Part I · Order and Information

Lesson 03

What an algorithm is able to find out

Where we are

We can judge a proposed answer: it must hold the same items and sit in non-decreasing order. But an algorithm never gets to make that judgement about its own output — it has to build the output from nothing, and it starts out knowing nothing about which arrangement is the right one. So the question we could not ask before: what is it actually allowed to ask, and how much does an answer give it?

1 The algorithm does not see the input

When you look at the sequence 17, 4, 29, 4, 11, you take it in at a glance. It is tempting to imagine an algorithm doing something similar — looking at the whole thing and then getting to work.

It does not. An algorithm has no eyes. It has operations: a fixed list of things it is able to do, each one returning a definite result. Everything it will ever know is assembled out of the results of operations it chose to perform.

That is not a limitation we are imposing to make the problem interesting. It is what a computation is. And it means that before we can say anything at all about how much work sorting takes, we have to say which operations are on the list. Change the list and you change the problem — not how hard it is to solve, but what solving it even involves.

Definition

A computational model is a statement of which operations an algorithm may perform and what each one costs. It fixes what is knowable and what is expensive.

Every claim in the rest of this series is made relative to some model. A claim without one is not a weak claim; it is not a claim at all.

2 The weakest honest assumption

So which operations should we allow? We could allow a great many, but there is a good reason to start with almost none.

Think about what we actually need in order to sort. Lesson 00 found the atom: take two items, ask which comes first. That single operation is enough to build any ordering knowledge at all, and it is available for every kind of item — numbers, names, dates, records, things with no numbers in them anywhere. Anything you can order, you can compare.

So we begin by assuming that and nothing else. The items are sealed: we cannot read them, add them, look at their digits or ask how big they are. We can hand any two of them to the relation and receive one of two answers.

Definition

In the comparison model, the items are opaque. The only operation available is a comparison: given two items $a$ and $b$, learn whether $a \preceq b$. We count comparisons and ignore everything else.

Two things to be clear about, because both get misremembered.

This is a choice, not a discovery. Nothing in the nature of sorting forces it. We are choosing to see how far the weakest reasonable assumption takes us, precisely because whatever we prove under it will hold no matter what the items turn out to be.

And it is a weak assumption, which is exactly what makes results proved under it strong. A limit established here applies to sorting numbers, sorting strings, sorting anything — because none of those were assumed. In Part XI we will hand the algorithm more power and watch the limit move.A general habit worth forming: when you prove something, notice how much you assumed. A result that needs little applies widely. A result that needs a lot may be true and still be almost useless.

3 Watching knowledge accumulate

Sealed items and one operation. That sounds impoverished, and the point of this section is that it is less impoverished than it sounds — for a reason we have already met and not yet cashed in.

Figure 3.1 gives you five sealed boxes and lets you spend comparisons on them. Every question you ask is recorded, and so is everything that follows from what you have asked.

Ask three or four questions and look at the grid. You will find cells filled in that you never asked about.

Those are the ones you got for nothing. If you learned that box B comes before box D, and separately that D comes before A, then B comes before A — and you were never asked to pay for it. The relation carries that conclusion whether or not anybody checks.

This is transitivity from Lesson 01, showing up for the third time and now with a price tag attached. There it was what made a row possible at all. Here it is a discount: some of what you know was deduced rather than purchased.

Worth holding on to

An algorithm’s knowledge is not the list of answers it received. It is everything that follows from those answers. The two are very different sizes.

That gap is where every clever sorting algorithm lives. An algorithm that asks well arranges for each answer to imply a great deal; one that asks badly pays for facts it already had. We are not ready to make that precise — but from here on, whenever an algorithm seems to be doing suspiciously little work, the question to ask is what it is getting for free.

4 What a single answer does not settle

Now the other direction. The discount is real, and it is easy to overrate it.

Go back to Figure 3.1, reset it, and ask exactly one question. Suppose the answer is that box A comes before box B.

What do you know?

You know the relative position of two boxes. That is the entire content of the answer. You do not know where either sits among the other three. You do not know whether A is the smallest or merely smaller than B. You do not know a single value, and you will never know a single value, because the model does not contain an operation that would tell you one.

With five items there are ten pairs. One question has settled one of them, and possibly one or two more by chaining if you had earlier answers to chain with — starting from nothing, it settles exactly one.

So the honest summary of a comparison is this. It is the smallest unit of order knowledge there is, it can pay off far beyond itself once other answers are in hand, and on its own it is very nearly nothing.

Which sets up the question this part of the course exists to answer. If one comparison is very nearly nothing, and a complete arrangement is a great deal, how many comparisons does it take? We cannot attempt that yet, because we have no way to measure the size of “a complete arrangement.” That measurement is the next lesson.

5 The model is a choice, and choices can be changed

One last thing before we move on, because it is the hinge that Part XI turns on and it costs nothing to see now.

We chose to seal the boxes. Suppose we unseal them — suppose the items are numbers, and the algorithm is allowed one more operation: read the value in a box. Figure 5.1 gives you the switch.

The difference is not that reading is faster. It is that a read and a comparison are answers to different questions, and they carry different amounts. A comparison tells you about a pair. A read tells you about an item, and an item participates in every pair it belongs to.

We are not going to build anything on this yet, and it would be easy to draw the wrong conclusion from it — reading values does not automatically make sorting cheaper, and the lesson where we finally cash this in is a long way off. Register only the shape of the observation:

The hinge

Everything we are about to prove is a statement about the comparison model. When an algorithm later appears to beat one of those results, it will not have broken anything. It will be working in a different model — and the first question to ask of it is always which assumption changed?

The idea in one line

An algorithm knows only what its operations return and what follows from that — so before asking how hard a problem is, ask what the algorithm is allowed to do. Fix the operations and you have fixed the problem.

Check yourself

You compare five sealed items and receive four answers: A before B, C before D, B before C, and A before E. How many of the ten pairs do you now know about?

Seven of the ten. Four you paid for, three more for free.

Chaining A before B with B before C gives A before C. Chaining that with C before D gives A before D. Chaining B before C with C before D gives B before D. That is three deductions, none of them costing a question.

The three still unknown all involve E. You know A comes before E, and nothing else about it — E never appeared in a chain with B, C or D, so its position relative to them is completely open.

The shape to notice: four questions bought seven facts, and the item you asked about once is the item you know least about. Where you spend your questions decides what you get for free.

We said the comparison model is a weak assumption, and then said that makes results proved under it strong. Is that backwards?

It sounds backwards and it is exactly right. Keeping the two straight is worth the effort, because the confusion recurs everywhere.

A weak assumption is one that is easy to satisfy — few demands, so many situations meet it. The comparison model demands only that items can be compared, which is true of numbers, strings, dates, records, and anything else you might want to sort.

A result proved from weak assumptions therefore applies everywhere those weak assumptions hold, which is nearly everywhere. That breadth is what makes it strong.

Flip it around to see the trade. If we assumed the items were integers between 1 and 100, we could prove sharper things — but each would come with the condition attached, and would say nothing about sorting names. Stronger assumptions buy sharper conclusions and narrower reach. There is no free lunch in either direction, and the whole of Part XI is an extended study of this exchange rate.

Someone claims: “sorting ten million items takes at least a second.” What is wrong with the claim as stated?

No model, so no claim.

At least a second on what? A statement about elapsed time depends on the machine, the language, how items are laid out in memory, whether comparing two of them is one instruction or a thousand. None of that has been specified, so the sentence cannot be shown true and cannot be shown false.

Repair it by naming a model and a count: in the comparison model, any algorithm sorting ten million items must make at least so many comparisons. That is checkable, provable, and machine-independent.

The price of the repair is honesty about what you have. A claim about comparisons is not a claim about seconds, and converting between them needs facts about a machine that no proof can supply. We hold that distinction all the way to Part XII, where we finally put the two side by side and find they disagree in interesting ways.

In the comparison model, could an algorithm ever determine the actual value stored in an item?

No — and the reason is worth more than the answer.

Suppose an algorithm claimed to. Take its input and replace every item with a different one, chosen so that every pair compares exactly the same way as before — replace 4, 11, 29 with 100, 200, 300, say. Every comparison the algorithm makes returns precisely the answer it returned on the original input, so the algorithm behaves identically, step for step, and announces the same values. It is now wrong.

So no such algorithm exists. And notice the method, because it is the one we will use to prove far harder things: build a second input the algorithm cannot distinguish from the first, and let it be wrong on one of them.

You have now seen that move twice — it was also the answer to the first question in Lesson 00. In Lesson 06 it grows into the argument that gives this whole part its conclusion.

What’s still unanswered

We have an operation, and we know roughly what it buys: one pair directly, sometimes several more by chaining, never a value. What we have no grip on at all is the size of the job.

“How many comparisons does sorting take?” is a question about a ratio — how much must be learned, divided by how much each question teaches. We have started on the denominator. The numerator is untouched: we have never asked how much there is to learn in the first place.

So that is next, and it turns out to be a counting problem. How many arrangements could the answer possibly be?
