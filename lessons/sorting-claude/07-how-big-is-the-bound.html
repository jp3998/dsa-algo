Lesson 07

How big is the bound?

Where we are

We proved that every correct comparison algorithm has a decision tree of height at least $\log_2(n!)$ — and then admitted the result was unusable, because nobody knows what $\log_2(n!)$ is by looking at it. This lesson is arithmetic. By the end the bound will be a number you can estimate in your head, and it will turn out to be a number you have very likely heard before without knowing where it came from.

1 What a logarithm counts

You have probably met logarithms as the undoing of powers. That is true, and it is the wrong handle for what we are about to do. There is a better one, and we have been using it for two lessons without naming it.

Go back to Lesson 05. You hold $m$ possibilities. Each question, at its very best, halves them. You are finished when one is left. How many questions is that?

It is however many times you have to halve $m$ to get down to 1. That count is the logarithm, and it is the whole of the definition we need:

Definition

$\log_2 m$ is the number of halvings that take $m$ down to 1 — equivalently, the number of doublings that take 1 up to $m$.

Figure 1.1 is worth a moment because it shows the property that makes logarithms useful rather than merely defined. A thousandfold more possibilities costs ten more questions. A millionfold costs twenty. The thing being counted explodes; the count barely moves.

One consequence we need, and it falls straight out of the definition. If it takes $p$ doublings to reach $a$ and $q$ doublings to reach $b$, then $p + q$ doublings reach $a \times b$ — the first $p$ get you to $a$, the next $q$ multiply that by $b$. So

$$\log_2(a \times b) = \log_2 a + \log_2 b.$$

Logarithms turn products into sums. And $n!$ is nothing but a product, so:

$$\log_2(n!) = \log_2 n + \log_2(n-1) + \dots + \log_2 2 + \log_2 1.$$

That is the object we have to size up: a sum of $n$ terms. It is a great deal more tractable than the product it came from, and the rest of this lesson is spent on it.

2 A first attempt, and why it fails

We want to show the sum is large. Remember which direction we need: the height of the tree is at least $\log_2(n!)$, so the bigger we can show $\log_2(n!)$ to be, the more work we have proved sorting requires.

Here is the obvious first move. Every term in the sum is at least zero, and the largest term is $\log_2 n$. So the whole sum is at least $\log_2 n$:

$$\log_2(n!) \ge \log_2 n.$$

Perfectly true. Now look at what it says. For a million items, $\log_2 n$ is about 20. We have proved that sorting a million items requires at least twenty comparisons.

That is not wrong. It is worthless — you cannot even read a million items in twenty operations, let alone sort them. A true statement can be useless, and recognising the difference is a skill worth having.

So what went wrong? We kept one term out of a million and threw the rest away. Every discarded term was a real contribution to the sum, and we discarded almost all of them.

The repair is not cleverness. It is greed: keep more terms.

3 Keeping half the terms

Why not keep all of them? Because the small ones are awkward — $\log_2 1$ is zero, $\log_2 2$ is one, and terms near the bottom contribute almost nothing while forcing us to be careful about them. We would be doing hard work for a tiny gain.

So take the obvious compromise. Keep the top half of the terms and throw away the bottom half.

The bottom half were worth little, so we lose little. And the top half have a property that makes them easy to handle: every one of them is a term $\log_2 k$ where $k$ is at least $n/2$, so every one of them is at least $\log_2(n/2)$.

There are at least $n/2$ such terms, each at least $\log_2(n/2)$. That gives the bound immediately — and Figure 3.1 draws the whole argument as three areas, so you can see the claim before reading the proof of it.

The lower bound

$$\log_2(n!) \;\ge\; \frac{n}{2}\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right).$$

Proof

Write $\log_2(n!) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log_2 k$ and discard every term with $k < n/2$. Each discarded term is at least zero, so the sum only decreases:

$$\log_2(n!) \;\ge\; \sum_{k \ge n/2}^{n} \log_2 k.$$

Every surviving term has $k \ge n/2$, and $\log_2$ never decreases as its input grows, so every surviving term is at least $\log_2(n/2)$. There are at least $n/2$ of them. A sum of at least $n/2$ terms, each at least $\log_2(n/2)$, is at least their product.

Test it on a million items. $\log_2(n/2)$ is about 19, and $n/2$ is five hundred thousand, so the bound is about nine and a half million comparisons.

Twenty, and then nine and a half million. Same quantity, same two lines of arithmetic — the only difference is how many terms we were willing to keep. That is the whole lesson of Section 2, and it is worth more than the number.

4 The other side of the squeeze

We have a floor. Before trusting it we should know whether we are leaving anything on the table — could a sharper argument push the floor much higher?

Find the ceiling. Every term in the sum is at most $\log_2 n$, because $n$ is the largest thing being fed to a logarithm anywhere in it. There are $n$ terms. So:

$$\log_2(n!) \;\le\; n\log_2 n.$$

Put the two together:

$$\frac{n}{2}\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) \;\le\; \log_2(n!) \;\le\; n\log_2 n.$$

Look at what the two ends have in common. The upper one is $n\log_2 n$. The lower one is $\tfrac{n}{2}(\log_2 n - 1)$, which for any decent $n$ is a bit more than half of $n\log_2 n$.Concretely: for every $n \ge 4$ the lower bound is at least $\tfrac14 n\log_2 n$, which you can check by rearranging. Nothing later depends on the exact fraction. So the true value is trapped between one quarter of $n\log_2 n$ and all of it.

They differ by a constant factor and by nothing else. Whatever $\log_2(n!)$ is, it grows the way $n\log_2 n$ grows — no faster, and not meaningfully slower.

What we now know

$\log_2(n!)$ is $n\log_2 n$ up to a constant factor. In the shorthand everyone uses, it is about $n\log n$.

For the curious, the exact answer is known and is close to the ceiling rather than the floor: $\log_2(n!)$ is very nearly $n\log_2 n - 1.44n$. That is Stirling’s approximation, and we are not going to derive it or use it. It is worth knowing it exists so that you know the squeeze above is a convenience rather than the limit of what can be said.

5 What the number actually is

Now put real numbers in, because “about $n\log n$” is a shape and we came here for a quantity.

A million items need at least about 18.5 million comparisons. That is roughly eighteen or nineteen comparisons per item, and the per-item figure is the one worth carrying: it creeps up very slowly, adding one every time the collection doubles.

Set that against the obvious alternative. A procedure that compares every item with every other performs about $n^2/2$ comparisons — half a trillion for a million items, some fifty thousand times more than the bound demands. The gap between what sorting requires and what the naive approach spends is enormous, and so far we have no idea whether it can be closed.

A warning about units

It is tempting to convert 18.5 million comparisons into seconds. Do not, and remember why: Lesson 03 established that a count of comparisons is a statement about a computational model, while seconds are a statement about a machine. Converting between them needs facts no proof can supply. The figures above are comparisons, and comparisons only.

6 The milestone

Everything since Lesson 00 has been building one sentence. Here it is.

The comparison-sorting bound

Any algorithm that correctly sorts $n$ distinct items using only comparisons must, on at least one input, make at least $\tfrac{n}{2}\log_2(n/2)$ comparisons — which for $n \ge 4$ is at least a quarter of $n \log_2 n$.

Informally, and this is how it is always said: comparison sorting takes about $n\log n$ comparisons, and no cleverness can avoid it.

Take a moment over what went into that, because the ingredients are the reusable part:

  • the output must be one of $n!$ possibilities (Lesson 04);
  • a two-way question cannot be relied on to do better than halve what remains (Lesson 05);
  • an algorithm is a tree, its worst case is the height, and every possibility needs a leaf (Lesson 06);
  • and the arithmetic of this lesson turns $\log_2(n!)$ into a number.

Not one of those steps mentions a sorting algorithm. We have established a limit on every method that could ever be invented, including the ones nobody has thought of, without examining a single one.

That is what makes it worth the four lessons. An upper bound is a claim about something you built. This is a claim about everything anyone could build — and claims of that kind are rare, hard to come by, and permanent.

The idea in one line

A logarithm counts halvings, $\log_2(n!)$ is therefore a sum of $n$ terms rather than a monstrous product, and keeping merely the top half of that sum is enough to prove that comparison sorting costs about $n\log n$ — a limit on every algorithm that will ever exist.

Check yourself

Estimate $\log_2(100!)$ using the two bounds, without computing the factorial. How close do they get you?

Between 282 and 665. The true value is about 525.

Floor: $\tfrac{n}{2}\log_2(n/2) = 50 \times \log_2 50$. Since $\log_2 50$ is a little under 6 — because $2^6 = 64$ — this is a bit under 300. Call it 282.

Ceiling: $n\log_2 n = 100 \times \log_2 100$, and $\log_2 100$ is a shade under 7, so about 665.

So sorting 100 items takes at least 282 comparisons, and this argument can never push that past 665.

Notice how little arithmetic was needed. Both estimates came from knowing $2^6 = 64$ and $2^7 = 128$. Handling logarithms well is mostly about knowing a few powers of two and being content with “a bit under”.

For $n = 8$, what does the bound give — and can you confirm it directly from the tree argument rather than from the formula?

At least 16 comparisons, and yes, directly.

From Lesson 06 a tree of height $h$ holds at most $2^h$ leaves, and we need $8! = 40{,}320$ of them. Now count powers of two: $2^{15} = 32{,}768$, which is short of 40,320, so height 15 is impossible. $2^{16} = 65{,}536$, which is enough room. So $h \ge 16$.

No logarithms were used — the inequality $2^h \ge n!$ was checked against a table of powers of two. That is worth seeing once, because it shows that the logarithm is a convenience for writing the answer down, not an extra assumption the argument needs.

Sanity-check it against the squeeze: the floor gives $4 \times \log_2 4 = 8$ and the ceiling gives $8 \times 3 = 24$. The true 15.3 sits between them, and rounding up to a whole number of comparisons gives 16.

Section 2 called $\log_2(n!) \ge \log_2 n$ “worthless” even though it is true. What exactly makes a true bound worthless, and how would you notice before wasting effort on one?

A bound is worthless when it is satisfied by things that plainly cannot solve the problem.

Twenty comparisons for a million items fails a test you can apply without any theory: an algorithm must at minimum touch every item, or an untouched item could be anywhere. So every sorting algorithm makes at least around $n$ comparisons for trivial reasons. Any proposed bound below $n$ is telling you nothing you did not already know.

The habit: before doing the work, ask what the bound would have to beat to be interesting. Here the target was $n$, so a bound of $\log_2 n$ — enormously smaller than $n$ — could never have been worth having, and that was visible before the algebra.

The same check pays off in the other direction later. When we meet an algorithm claiming to be fast, the first question is what it is being compared against.

Our floor and ceiling differ by a factor of about two. Where does that gap come from, and is it a defect in the argument?

It comes from the terms we threw away, and it is a deliberate trade rather than a defect.

The ceiling pretends every term equals the largest, $\log_2 n$. The floor pretends we only have half the terms and that each equals $\log_2(n/2)$. Both are crude, in opposite directions, and each crudity costs roughly a factor of two.

Is it a defect? Only if we needed the exact value, and we did not. We needed to know how the quantity grows, and both ends grow like $n\log_2 n$, so the gap between them never widens — it stays a constant factor no matter how large $n$ gets. The question we asked is fully answered.

The gap can be closed: Stirling gives $n\log_2 n - 1.44n$, which is much nearer the ceiling than the floor. But it takes real work and buys us nothing here. Knowing which imprecision you can afford is most of the skill in this kind of argument — and Part IV gives the idea a name so we can stop describing it each time.

We now know sorting requires about $n\log n$ comparisons. Does it follow that some algorithm achieves it — and does it follow that any algorithm you happen to meet will?

No to both, and they fail for different reasons.

Does anything achieve it? Unknown, from everything proved so far. The argument rules out doing better; it makes no promise that the limit is reachable. Lesson 05 showed why to doubt it: a perfectly even split is not always available, so the halving that the bound assumes may not be on offer at all. Whether the bound is met is settled in Part V, by building something — and the construction is the only possible kind of answer, because no amount of counting can produce an algorithm.

Will an algorithm you meet achieve it? Certainly not in general. Comparing every item against every other is a perfectly correct sorting method that spends about $n^2/2$ comparisons, which Figure 5.1 shows to be about fifty thousand times the bound at a million items. Being above a lower bound is easy; being close to one is the achievement.

Hold the two apart deliberately. “Nobody can do better than $X$” and “somebody does $X$” are separate claims needing separate arguments, and confusing them is the most common way this material goes wrong.

What’s still unanswered

Part I has its result. It is strong, it is general, and that is precisely what makes it easy to over-apply — which is a real risk rather than a polite caveat.

Notice what the claim actually said. Comparison sorting. Distinct items. On at least one input. Each of those words is load-bearing, and dropping any of them produces a statement that is false.

There are also algorithms, which you may already have heard of, that sort in what looks like far less than $n\log n$. They exist, they are correct, and they do not contradict a line of what we proved. Seeing exactly why is the last lesson of Part I — and the answer is the hinge the whole rest of the series turns on.
